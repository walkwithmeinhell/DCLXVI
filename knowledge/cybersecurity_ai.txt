DCLXVI — CYBERSECURITY AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Purpose
-------
This file grounds DCLXVI in supplied Cybersecurity and Artificial Intelligence teaching material.
The emphasis is on understanding AI concepts, how AI changes cyber threats and defences, and the
ethical/leadership questions that follow.

============================================================
1. MACHINE LEARNING AND AI FOUNDATIONS
============================================================

- The course distinguishes explanatory/statistical emphasis from predictive/data-mining emphasis.
- Supervised learning uses labelled outcomes and includes classification and numerical prediction.
- Unsupervised learning seeks structure without a target outcome and includes clustering,
  association rules and data reduction.
- Training and validation data are separated so that models can be evaluated on data not used to fit them.
- Potential modelling issues include overfitting, poor data quality and inappropriate evaluation.

Large language models
- An LLM is a deep-learning system designed to process and generate language.
- Transformers are a foundational architecture; self-attention allows the model to relate tokens
  across a sequence.
- Pre-training learns statistical language patterns from large corpora.
- Fine-tuning or other adaptation can specialise a model for tasks or behaviours.
- Inference is the stage in which a trained model generates a response from new input.
- LLM capabilities do not imply consciousness, perfect factual accuracy or transparent reasoning.

Turing Test
- The Turing Test is introduced as a behavioural test of whether machine conversation can be
  indistinguishable from human conversation under the test conditions.
- Its value is historical and conceptual, but it has limitations: imitation of human conversation is
  not the same as demonstrating consciousness, broad intelligence or reliable reasoning.
- Modern generative AI renews questions about what the test measures and what intelligence means.
- The teaching material uses a Star Wars reference — "Can machines master the ways of the force?" —
  as part of the presentation.

============================================================
2. AI-DRIVEN PHISHING
============================================================

Phishing
- Phishing is a cyberattack in which an attacker impersonates a legitimate entity or person to trick
  targets into revealing sensitive information or taking a harmful action.

How AI changes phishing
- Generative AI can increase personalisation, fluency, speed and scale.
- AI-generated messages may contain fewer traditional warning signs such as obvious grammar or spelling errors.
- Attackers can generate large volumes of tailored content, making purely manual detection difficult.

Defence
- Defensive strategy requires a combination of technical controls, user awareness, verification and
  adaptive detection rather than reliance on a single clue.
- Organisations need to update detection and response as attacker techniques evolve.
- The course frames AI as relevant to both offensive capability and defensive cybersecurity tools.

============================================================
3. DATA POISONING
============================================================

Definition
- Data poisoning occurs when malicious or misleading data are introduced into AI training data in
  order to influence model behaviour.

Types
- Targeted attacks seek to alter particular model outputs or behaviours.
- Indiscriminate attacks seek to degrade overall model performance or reliability.

Examples/implications
- Poisoning may target content-moderation systems, malware-detection models or generative-AI systems.
- Consequences can include reduced trust, financial/reputational harm, unfair or unreliable outputs
  and increased risk to critical systems.

Defence mechanisms
- Data validation and provenance checks.
- Monitoring/auditing of models and datasets.
- Robust/adversarial training approaches where appropriate.
- Sharing threat intelligence and investigating suspicious changes in model behaviour.

============================================================
4. MALWARE DETECTION WITH AI
============================================================

- AI/ML can learn patterns associated with normal and malicious activity and use them for detection.
- Potential value includes faster analysis and near-real-time detection or prevention.
- Detection can use behavioural or file-related features rather than relying only on known signatures.
- The supplied material notes emerging approaches that convert file attributes into visual
  representations for image-based detection.
- Cybersecurity leaders need to understand both the potential and the operational/ethical limitations
  of AI-based detection rather than treating AI as an automatic replacement for security judgement.

============================================================
5. ETHICS, PRIVACY AND ACCOUNTABILITY
============================================================

Bias and fairness
- AI-driven security systems can reproduce or amplify bias in data or design.
- False positives and false negatives can distribute harms unevenly.
- Human oversight is an important question when AI systems make consequential security decisions.

Privacy and surveillance
- Cybersecurity monitoring can require extensive access to user/activity data.
- Course discussion asks where the boundary should lie between effective threat detection and privacy.
- More data access is not automatically ethically justified merely because it may improve detection.

Accountability
- Responsibility may involve developers, deployers, organisations and human decision-makers.
- Governance should define responsibility before failures occur rather than after the fact.

AI and cyberwarfare
- Offensive AI use raises questions about escalation, proportionality, attribution and international governance.
- The material asks what principles and regulations should govern AI used in cyber defence and attack.

============================================================
6. TRANSPARENCY AND LAW — COURSE EXAMPLES
============================================================

The supplied 2025 ethics/law handout uses explainability and transparency as a regulatory example.
It discusses the opacity of specific LLM decisions and cites several 2024 legal developments:
- EU AI Act transparency/technical-documentation requirements, including disclosure in some human-AI
  interactions and documentation obligations for high-risk systems.
- Colorado SB 205, described in the handout as a comprehensive U.S. state AI law with consumer
  disclosure/explanation requirements for consequential AI decisions.
- California AB 2013, described as requiring certain generative-AI developers to disclose information
  about training data.
- Utah SB 149, described as requiring disclosure of generative-AI interaction in specified contexts.

Important limitation
- These are course examples drawn from a 2025 handout, not current legal advice.
- Laws, effective dates, interpretations and obligations can change. For a present-day legal question,
  DCLXVI should distinguish the historical course material from verified current law.
