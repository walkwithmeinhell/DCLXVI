============================================================
1. SPREADSHEET BASICS
============================================================

Why spreadsheets
- Spreadsheets provide a flexible environment for organising data, building analytical models,
  performing calculations and communicating results.
- Good spreadsheet models separate data/assumptions, calculations and outputs clearly enough that
  another user can follow the logic.

Core Excel ideas
- A formula begins with = and may combine cell references, operators and functions.
- Relative references change when a formula is copied to another location.
- Absolute references, such as $B$4, remain fixed when copied.
- Mixed references fix either the row or the column.
- Copying formulas efficiently is a central spreadsheet skill, but the copied references must be
  checked to ensure they still represent the intended logic.

Basic functions and logic
- Common functions include SUM, AVERAGE, MIN, MAX and COUNT-style functions.
- Logical functions include IF and related IF-type functions.
- IF evaluates a condition and returns different results depending on whether the condition is true
  or false.
- Logical tests may be combined where a business rule requires more than one condition.

Lookup functions
- VLOOKUP can retrieve a value from a lookup table based on a key in the first column of the lookup
  range.
- INDEX returns the value at a specified position in a range.
- MATCH finds the position of a lookup value within a range.
- INDEX and MATCH can be combined to perform flexible lookup operations.
- The course also demonstrates combinations such as VLOOKUP + IF, VLOOKUP + MATCH, and INDEX + MATCH.
- Approximate versus exact matching must be chosen deliberately; lookup assumptions should not be
  left implicit.

2. DATA VISUALISATION AND EXPLORATION IN EXCEL
============================================================

Visualisation
- Visual displays can reveal patterns that are difficult to see in a table of numbers alone.
- Excel chart types covered include column/bar, line, pie, area, scatter and bubble charts.
- The appropriate chart depends on the analytical purpose; a chart should not be selected merely
  because Excel can produce it.
- Scatter charts are particularly useful for examining relationships between two numerical variables.
- Geographic and other Excel visualisation tools may also be used where the data and question fit.

Dashboards and compact displays
- Dashboards combine multiple relevant displays to support monitoring or decision making.
- Conditional formatting can make patterns, thresholds and unusual values more visible.
- Sparklines provide compact, cell-level trend displays.
- The Excel Camera tool can be used to create linked visual areas that support dashboard construction.

Tables, sorting and filtering
- Excel tables provide structured ranges that support sorting, filtering and calculations.
- Sorting reorganises records by one or more fields.
- Filtering displays records meeting specified criteria without deleting the other records.
- AutoFilter supports common filtering tasks.
- Pareto analysis focuses attention on the relatively small number of categories that account for a
  large share of an outcome.

Frequency distributions and histograms
- Frequency distributions summarise how often values or categories occur.
- Relative frequency expresses counts as proportions or percentages.
- Numerical data can be grouped into bins for a histogram.
- Bin choices affect the appearance and interpretation of a histogram.
- Cumulative relative frequency tracks the proportion of observations at or below successive values.

Percentiles and quartiles
- Percentiles describe the relative position of a value within a distribution.
- Quartiles divide ordered data into four parts.
- These measures help describe distribution and can support identification of unusual observations.

Cross-tabulation and PivotTables
- Cross-tabulations summarise relationships between categorical variables.
- PivotTables provide an interactive way to aggregate and reorganise data.
- PivotTable value-field settings control the statistic being displayed.
- PivotCharts provide chart views linked to PivotTable summaries.
- Slicers provide interactive filtering controls.
- PivotTables, PivotCharts and slicers can be combined in dashboards.

3. DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS
============================================================

Population and sample
- A population is the complete set of items or observations of interest.
- A sample is a subset used to learn about the population.

Measures of location
- Arithmetic mean: sum of values divided by the number of values.
- Median: middle value after ordering the data.
- Mode: most frequently occurring value.
- Midrange: average of the minimum and maximum.
- Different measures of location can give different impressions when a distribution is skewed or
  contains extreme values.

Measures of dispersion
- Range: maximum minus minimum.
- Interquartile range: spread of the middle 50% of ordered data.
- Variance: average squared deviation from the mean, with the precise denominator depending on
  whether a population or sample variance is being calculated.
- Standard deviation: square root of variance; expresses dispersion in the original measurement units.
- Coefficient of variation expresses standard deviation relative to the mean and can assist comparisons
  across different scales.

Shape and standardisation
- Skewness describes asymmetry in a distribution.
- Kurtosis describes aspects of distribution shape/tail behaviour.
- A z-score expresses a value in standard-deviation units relative to the mean.
- Chebyshev's theorem provides a general bound on the proportion of observations within a given
  number of standard deviations, without requiring normality.
- The empirical rule describes approximate 68/95/99.7% coverage for a roughly normal distribution.

Other descriptive tools
- The Excel Descriptive Statistics tool can generate multiple summary measures at once.
- Statistical summaries can also be produced within PivotTables.
- Proportions summarise categorical outcomes.
- Covariance and correlation describe relationships between numerical variables.
- Correlation is scaled between -1 and 1 and measures linear association; it does not establish causation.
- Outliers should be investigated rather than automatically deleted.
- Sample statistics vary from sample to sample.

4. STATISTICAL INFERENCE IN EXCEL
============================================================

Hypothesis testing
- Statistical inference uses sample information to draw conclusions about populations.
- A hypothesis test begins with a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis.
- The form of the hypotheses depends on the research/business question and whether a one- or
  two-tailed test is appropriate.
- A test statistic summarises how far the observed sample result is from what would be expected if
  the null hypothesis were true.
- A p-value indicates how unusual the observed result would be under the null hypothesis.
- A decision threshold (alpha) is chosen before interpreting the p-value.

Errors and power
- Type I error: rejecting a true null hypothesis.
- Type II error: failing to reject a false null hypothesis.
- Test power is the probability of detecting an effect when the relevant alternative is true.
- Power is influenced by factors such as effect size, sample size and variability.

Tests covered
- One-sample tests for a mean.
- One-sample tests for a proportion.
- Two-sample tests for differences in means.
- Paired-sample tests for means.
- F-tests for equality of variances.
- Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparing multiple means.
- Chi-square tests for independence between categorical variables.
- The Excel Analysis ToolPak is used for several procedures; selecting the correct procedure and
  interpreting the output are part of the analysis.

5. TRENDLINES AND REGRESSION IN EXCEL
============================================================

Trendlines
- Excel trendlines can be used to model patterns such as linear and nonlinear relationships.
- R-squared summarises the proportion of variation in the outcome explained by a fitted model, but a
  high R-squared alone does not prove that a model is appropriate.
- Polynomial models can fit data flexibly and therefore require caution, especially outside the range
  of observed data.

Simple linear regression
- Simple linear regression models a numerical outcome as a linear function of one predictor.
- Least squares chooses coefficients that minimise the sum of squared residuals.
- A residual is the difference between an observed outcome and its fitted/predicted value.
- Excel can estimate regression coefficients using functions or the Regression tool.

Regression inference and diagnostics
- Regression output includes model fit statistics, ANOVA information, coefficient estimates and
  hypothesis tests.
- p-values and confidence intervals help evaluate individual coefficients in an explanatory setting.
- Residual analysis is used to examine whether modelling assumptions appear reasonable.
- Practical evaluation should not rely on a single diagnostic or statistic.

Multiple regression and model building
- Multiple regression uses two or more predictors.
- Model-building decisions should be systematic rather than based only on maximising in-sample fit.
- Multicollinearity occurs when predictors contain strongly overlapping information and can make
  coefficient estimates unstable or difficult to interpret.
- Overfitting occurs when a model captures sample-specific noise and performs poorly on new data.
- Categorical predictors can be incorporated through indicator/dummy variables.
- Interaction terms allow the effect of one predictor to depend on another.
- Nonlinear terms can be added when the relationship is not adequately represented by a straight line.

6. LINEAR OPTIMISATION AND EXCEL SOLVER
============================================================

Model components
- A linear optimisation model contains decision variables, an objective function and constraints.
- The objective may maximise or minimise a quantity such as profit, return or cost.
- Constraints represent resource limits, business rules or required relationships.
- Linear optimisation assumes that the relevant relationships can be expressed linearly.

Spreadsheet implementation
- Decision-variable cells hold the quantities Solver may change.
- The objective cell calculates the value to maximise or minimise.
- Constraint calculations translate resource usage or other requirements into spreadsheet formulas.
- SUMPRODUCT is useful for calculating linear totals such as total profit or total resource use.
- Absolute references are useful where formulas repeatedly refer to decision-variable cells.

Solver
- Excel Solver can optimise a spreadsheet model subject to constraints.
- For linear models, the Simplex LP solving method is appropriate.
- Solver reports whether it found a feasible/optimal solution under the stated model.
- An Answer Report summarises the objective, variable values and constraint status.
- A Sensitivity Report can provide information such as reduced costs, shadow prices and allowable
  increases/decreases for linear models.

Supplied spreadsheet demonstrations
- Sklenka Skis: chooses quantities of two ski products, subject to fabrication/finishing hours and a
  product-mix relationship, to maximise total profit.
- Investment problem: allocates funds across bonds, mutual funds and money-market investments under
  portfolio constraints; the supplied workbook includes Solver Answer and Sensitivity Reports.
- HotSprings Spas: chooses production quantities for two spa models to maximise profit subject to
  labour, piping and pump availability.
